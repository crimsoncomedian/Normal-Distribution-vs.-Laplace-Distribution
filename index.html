Standard Deviation vs. Mean Absolute Deviation

Both measure spread, but they treat distances from the mean differently. Explore how squared and absolute deviations are connected to two important probability models.

1. Two ways to measure deviations

Mean absolute deviation (MAD)

MAD=∑i=1N|xi−μ|N

Absolute deviation increases linearly with distance from the mean.

Standard deviation (SD)

σ=∑i=1N(xi−μ)2N

Squared deviation increases quadratically with distance from the mean.

3. The formulas behind the curves

Normal: squared deviations

f(x)=1σ2πe−(x−μ)22σ2

Its scale parameter is standard deviation σ.

Laplace: absolute deviations

f(x)=12be−|x−μ|b

Its scale parameter b equals the population MAD about μ; its SD is b√2.

Both curves in the graph have the same standard deviation so that their shapes can be compared fairly. That means the Laplace parameter is set to b=σ2.

4. Why does this matter?

  • Squaring deviations penalizes distant values more heavily than taking absolute values.
  • The mean minimizes the sum of squared deviations, while the median minimizes the sum of absolute deviations.
  • Normal-error models lead naturally to least-squares methods; Laplace-error models lead naturally to least-absolute-deviations methods.

Important: Computing SD does not make a dataset normally distributed, and computing MAD does not make it Laplace distributed. The curves are models based on assumptions about how values are distributed; SD and MAD are ways to summarize spread.
